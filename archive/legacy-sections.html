Abstract

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models are a promising paradigm for field robotics, but their deployment is bottlenecked by strict edge SWaP-C constraints. Compression techniques such as quantization, distillation, and pruning are well-established for LLMs and VLMs. Pruning, however, remains particularly underexplored for VLA models. We surprisingly find that pruning VLA models yields drastic performance degradation and increased safety violations. Through spectral analysis, we identify structural properties of VLA weights that help explain this fragility. Based on this insight, we introduce GLUESTICK, a post-pruning recovery method that restores much of the original model’s capability while preserving sparsity benefits. GLUESTICK computes a one-time corrective term by interpolating between the dense and pruned models in weight space. At inference, each pruned layer applies this correction to recover lost performance with minimal overhead. Our approach requires no additional training and is agnostic to the pruning algorithm. Across diverse VLA architectures and domains, GLUESTICK recovers up to 100% of the success rate and restores safety to near-dense levels, while saving up to 5 GB of memory and achieving up to 1.75× speedup.

Pruning collapses VLA task success and safety

Pruning that reduces LLaMA-2-70B’s mean accuracy by only 2.94% takes OpenVLA and NaVILA to 0.0% task success.

At 50% 2:4 structured sparsity with Magnitude or Wanda, task success falls from 85.2% (OpenVLA) and 43.0% (NaVILA) to 0.0%; OpenVLA’s unsafe-episode rate rises from 33.4% to 51.6% and NaVILA’s from 23.0% to 100.0%.

Why pruning breaks VLAs

In LLM and VLM models, the spectra are more anisotropic, evidenced by a steep initial drop followed by a long tail, which concentrates energy in a few dominant directions. VLA layers show a noticeably flatter decay, indicating energy spread across many directions, so pruning discards useful signal distributed throughout the matrix.

Method

GLUESTICK computes a one-time corrective term by interpolating between the dense and pruned models in weight space. At inference, each pruned layer applies this correction to recover lost performance with minimal overhead.

Offline: pruning Drag to rotate

1. Dense linear layer

Each linear layer \(W_\mathrm{dense}\) of the VLA is handled independently; 93–98% of the parameters of OpenVLA, WorldVLA and NaVILA are in linear layers.

2. Pruning to 2:4 sparsity

Magnitude or Wanda pruning zeroes two of every four weights, the pattern NVIDIA’s Sparse Tensor Cores accelerate. \(W_\mathrm{pruned}\) is left unchanged.

3. Gap matrix

Offline, \(W_\mathrm{gap} = W_\mathrm{dense} - W_\mathrm{pruned}\) captures the information lost to pruning.

4. Truncated SVD

The top \(r\) singular components of the gap give two small factors, \(A = U_r\Sigma_r\) and \(B = V_r\), stored next to the pruned weights.

5. Inference

\(h(x) = W_\mathrm{pruned}\,x + A(B^\top x)\): the sparse matmul is unchanged and the correction costs \(\mathcal{O}((d_\mathrm{in}+d_\mathrm{out})\,r)\).

GLUESTICK requires only the original dense model and its pruned counterpart, incurs a one-time offline cost, and is agnostic to the pruning algorithm. The rank r is its single hyperparameter; corrections are stored in Int8.

Equations and pseudocode

For each linear layer with dense weights \(W_\mathrm{dense}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_\mathrm{out}\times d_\mathrm{in}}\) and its pruned version \(W_\mathrm{pruned}\) (fixed, preserving the 2:4/4:8 pattern), define the gap matrix and take its truncated SVD, keeping the top \(r\) singular components:

\[ W_\mathrm{gap} = W_\mathrm{dense} - W_\mathrm{pruned}, \qquad W_\mathrm{gap} = U\Sigma V^\top \approx U_r \Sigma_r V_r^\top, \qquad A = U_r\Sigma_r \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\mathrm{out}\times r},\quad B = V_r \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\mathrm{in}\times r}. \]

At inference, each pruned layer adds the correction:

\[ h(x) = \underbrace{W_\mathrm{pruned}\,x}_{\text{efficient sparse matmul}} + \underbrace{B^\top x}_{\mathcal{O}(d_\mathrm{in} r)} \;\rightarrow\; \underbrace{A(\,\cdot\,)}_{\mathcal{O}(d_\mathrm{out} r)}. \]

The correction adds only \((d_\mathrm{in}+d_\mathrm{out})\,r\) parameters per layer, small compared with \(d_\mathrm{in}d_\mathrm{out}\) when \(r \ll \min\{d_\mathrm{in}, d_\mathrm{out}\}\). \(U_r\Sigma_r V_r^\top\) is the best rank-\(r\) approximation of the gap in Frobenius norm (Eckart–Young). We write GLUESTICK-\(r\) for a given rank; unless stated otherwise, GLUESTICK means GLUESTICK-500. Corrections are stored in Int8.

# Compute and store GLUESTICK correction terms for every linear layer

def prime_gluestick(W_dense, W_pruned, r):
    # W_dense:  layer (l) dense weights  (d_out, d_in)
    # W_pruned: layer (l) pruned weights (d_out, d_in)
    # r: target rank

    W_gap = W_dense - W_pruned
    U, S, Vh = torch.linalg.svd(W_gap)

    U_r = U[:, :r]
    S_r = S[:r]
    V_r = Vh[:r, :].T
    A   = U_r * S_r.unsqueeze(0)
    B   = V_r

    return {"A": A, "B": B}
class GLUESTICKWrap(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, pruned_linear_layer, A, B):
        super().__init__()
        self.pruned_linear = pruned_linear_layer
        self.A = A
        self.B = B

    def forward(self, x):
        # Efficient sparse matmul
        y = F.linear(x, self.pruned_linear.weight,
                     self.pruned_linear.bias)
        # Compute GLUESTICK correction
        correction = self.A @ (self.B.T @ x)
        return torch.add(y, correction)

# Load pruned model and correction terms, wrap every pruned linear layer
model = load_pruned_model()
correction_terms = load_correction_terms()
model = apply_gluestick(model, correction_terms)

Additional analyses

Spectral change under fine-tuning

OpenVLA’s backbone is a fine-tuned LLaMA-2, so the spectra compare the same layers before and after VLA fine-tuning. Measured as AUC/k (higher = slower decay), fine-tuning shifts the language backbone from 1.32 to 1.55 and the vision encoders from 0.84 to 0.92.

Ranking layers by their AUC/k change, pruning the top, middle and bottom 50 layers hurts success most, moderately and least, respectively. On LIBERO Object, GLUESTICK recovers 16.7% for the top-50 layers but only 3.3% for the bottom 50, suggesting layers with larger spectral change benefit more from correction.

VLA component sensitivity

Pruning only the language or only the vision backbone while keeping the rest dense: for OpenVLA (7.5B parameters; 89.4% language, 9.7% vision, 0.9% projector) language pruning costs −54.0% on LIBERO Spatial and vision pruning −27.8%, but per million parameters vision pruning is 4.75× more damaging. For NaVILA (8.5B; 94.5% / 5.0% / 0.4%) the drops are −20.0% and −40.0%, 37.5× more damaging per parameter for vision.

Vision backbones are disproportionately sensitive while offering little memory benefit, so the main evaluations focus on pruning the language backbone.

Weight-only decomposition without pruning

Replacing OpenVLA weights with a low-rank SVD approximation (no pruning) achieves 0% success on LIBERO Spatial at nearly every rank. The pruned weight matrix retains structure that low-rank approximation alone cannot capture; GLUESTICK keeps the pruned weights (and their sparsity benefits) and uses SVD only to reintroduce lost directions.

Calibration set selection

Wanda’s baseline pruning of OpenVLA’s language backbone used 15K states from the LIBERO fine-tuning corpus. A smaller, targeted set of 3.5K states within a 5% window around the first gripper-closing event improved the pruned backbone’s LIBERO Spatial success from 31.2% to 33.4%, a gain of about 2% from a calibration set less than a quarter of the size.

Singular value selection and an activation-aware variant

All reported results use the top-r singular components by magnitude. Choosing singular values uniformly at random (GLUESTICK-200 on a fully sparse language backbone) recovers only about 10% of the task success of magnitude-based selection.

An activation-aware variant scales the gap by Wanda’s calibration activation norms before the SVD. The recovered subspace changes (cosine similarity 0.86 at rank 500, 0.78 at rank 200), but it performs slightly worse on LIBERO Spatial: 59.0% vs 60.2% at rank 500 and 47.6% vs 49.0% at rank 200, with nearly identical offline cost (2.375 vs 2.366 s per layer) and unchanged inference cost.

Manipulation vs. navigation tolerance

Manipulation needs centimeter-level control, so tiny trajectory deviations can cause complete failure, whereas navigation tolerates multi-meter deviations. The average path-length difference between successful and failed episodes is 0.018 m in manipulation versus 3.292 m in navigation.

GLUESTICK and LoRA

Both add a low-rank term to a layer’s output, but LoRA’s matrices are learned by gradient-based training on a dataset for efficient fine-tuning, while GLUESTICK’s are computed analytically, with no training at all, from the exact gap between the dense and pruned weights for post-pruning recovery. The source of the injected information and the problem being solved are entirely different.

Safety metrics and limitations

Navigation safety: a collision is recorded whenever the agent’s actions cause unintended contact with objects. Manipulation safety: five metrics covering joint-limit violations (within 0.1% of limits), arm collisions, unsafe object velocities (above 1.0 m/s), end-effector containment (more than 5% outside the workspace region) and whole-body containment (more than 1% outside). An episode is unsafe if it violates any metric.

Limitations. The single hyperparameter introduces an inherent trade-off between success, memory and latency, and a uniform rank is applied across all layers. Future work includes reducing this trade-off and exploring adaptive rank selection across layers.
